# The Landscape: What Changed, and What It Means for You

> ~10 min read · For: everyone, especially Year 1 · Last reviewed: September 2026

**TL;DR**

- AI didn't remove the work of software engineering. It **compressed** it. First drafts are cheap now, but judgment, debugging and understanding are worth more.
- The entry-level market is **tighter than it was**, and the evidence is real. It is not closed. The students who get hired can show they understand what they build.
- In Sri Lanka, employers say the problem is **job-readiness, not the number of graduates**. That is good news, because job-readiness is something you can work on.

---

## What actually changed

Ten years ago, getting unstuck meant searching, reading five old answers, trying the one that almost fitted, and watching it break. That loop was slow, and **the slowness taught you**. By the time the code worked, you understood the problem.

Today you can get a working starting point in seconds, written for your exact problem. That is a real improvement. But the part that used to teach you, the struggle, is now optional. If you skip it every time, you finish tasks without learning from them.

That is the whole shift in one sentence: **the tool changed, but the responsibility didn't.**

Here is what AI now does well, and what it doesn't:

| AI is good at | Still needs a person |
|---|---|
| Boilerplate, first drafts, common patterns | Deciding *what* to build and *why* |
| Explaining an error message or unfamiliar code | Knowing when the explanation is wrong |
| Writing tests you describe | Deciding what is worth testing |
| Translating between languages and frameworks | Understanding the system the code runs in |
| Suggesting fixes | Debugging problems that span several parts of a system |
| Producing lots of code quickly | Being accountable when that code breaks in production |

Most junior tasks used to sit in the left column. That is why the entry-level market has moved, and why the right column is now where you need to be strong.

## The honest market picture

### Globally

Two data points are worth knowing:

- **Early-career developers have been hit first.** A Stanford study of US payroll data found that workers aged 22–25 in the most AI-exposed jobs, which include software developers, saw a **13–16% relative drop in employment** after generative AI spread. The exact figure depends on the version of the analysis. Older, more experienced workers in the same jobs were not affected ([Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen, Stanford Digital Economy Lab, 2025](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publications/canaries-in-the-coal-mine)). The authors' explanation is that the things junior workers know overlap most with what AI can already do.
- **Developers use AI heavily but don't trust it.** In the Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2025, **84%** of developers used or planned to use AI tools. But more of them *distrusted* the accuracy of AI output (**46%**) than trusted it (**33%**). The most common complaint was code that is "almost right, but not quite", and about **45%** said debugging AI-generated code takes longer ([Stack Overflow, 2025](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/ai)).

Put those together and a clear picture forms. Companies need fewer people who can only produce code. They still need people who can **tell whether code is right**, fix it when it isn't, and take responsibility for it.

### In Sri Lanka

Sri Lanka's software industry is mostly **export-focused**: companies building products and services for clients in the US, Europe, Australia and elsewhere. Three things matter for you:

- **The gap is job-readiness, not graduate numbers.** Industry bodies such as SLASSCOM have run programmes with universities specifically to close the gap between what degrees teach and what employers need ([Outsource Accelerator](https://news.outsourceaccelerator.com/slasscom-program-bridges-academia-industry/)). Employers keep saying the same thing: plenty of graduates, not enough who are ready to contribute.
- **Hiring is broader than "software engineer".** The busiest hiring areas include software engineering, **quality assurance, DevOps, data engineering and cyber security** ([COC Campus, 2026](https://www.coccampus.lk/it-career-opportunities-sri-lanka)). If you only look at one job title, you miss most of the market.
- **Experienced people leave, which opens space.** The IT sector has lost many experienced engineers and middle managers to migration ([Daily FT editorial](https://www.ft.lk/FT-View-Editorial/Challenging-prospects-for-IT-BPO-sector-amidst-brain-drain/58-761887)). *My opinion:* this is bad for the industry, but it means juniors who stay and grow quickly often get responsibility sooner than they would elsewhere.

You also compete in a **global** market now. Remote roles, overseas clients and open source mean a strong student in Kandy or Jaffna can be visible to the same employers as one in Colombo, or in another country. That works in both directions.

## The new junior bar

What does an employer want from a fresh graduate in 2026? Across job descriptions and hiring conversations, the answer is fairly consistent:

1. **Solid fundamentals.** You can explain how the code you ship works: data structures, databases, HTTP, and how a program uses memory. Covered in [Fundamentals that still matter](foundations/01-fundamentals-that-still-matter.md).
2. **Responsible AI fluency.** You use AI tools every day, you know their failure modes, and you can review what they produce. Postings increasingly ask for AI skills even at entry level ([The Week, 2026](https://www.theweek.in/news/sci-tech/2026/09/24/your-degree-may-not-be-enough-in-2027-why-employers-are-looking-for-ai-skills.html)).
3. **Proof, not claims.** You have projects that are deployed, tested and explained, not just listed on a CV. Covered in the Projects and portfolio guide (coming soon).
4. **Professional habits.** You use Git properly, write tests and readable code, and handle review feedback well. Covered in [Engineering habits](foundations/03-engineering-habits.md).
5. **Communication.** You can explain a trade-off to a teammate or a client in clear English. In an export industry, this matters a lot.

Notice what is *not* on the list: memorising every framework. New tools arrive every month. Nobody is caught up, including the senior engineers interviewing you. The patterns underneath (how agents loop, how data flows, how systems fail) change far more slowly. Learn those.

## Is it still worth it?

*My opinion:* yes, with open eyes.

Software is still being built. There is more of it than ever, and AI is producing more still. Someone has to design it, check it, run it, secure it and fix it. The job is shifting from *writing* code towards *owning* it. That is a harder job to enter, but it is a more interesting one, and it pays off for people who genuinely understand systems.

What is no longer worth it is the old shortcut of "finish the degree, learn one framework, and apply". That path is crowded, and AI competes on exactly those tasks.

## Where AI fits

- **Use AI** to explore this landscape. Ask it to summarise job descriptions, explain unfamiliar role titles, or compare career paths. It is a good research assistant.
- **Don't let AI decide for you.** It doesn't know the Sri Lankan market in detail, and it can be confidently wrong about salaries, companies and hiring processes. Check against real job posts and real people.
- **Read the guides in order of your worry.** If you're afraid AI will replace you, read [Learning with AI](foundations/02-learning-with-ai.md) next. It is the most important guide in this set.

## Do this next

- [ ] Collect **20 real job posts** for entry-level roles you might want, from local job boards, LinkedIn and company careers pages.
- [ ] Highlight the skills that appear in at least half of them. That is your real syllabus.
- [ ] Rate yourself honestly on the five points of the new junior bar, from 1 to 5.
- [ ] Pick the **one** lowest score and plan one month of work on it.
- [ ] Talk to one person who graduated 1–3 years ago and ask what their first year was really like.
- [ ] Skim the five career path titles in the [guide index](README.md) and mark two you want to learn more about.

---

## Build this

1. **Skills map (small).** Put your 20 job posts into a spreadsheet: role, company type, required skills, nice-to-have skills. Sort by frequency. You can ask AI to help you group the skills into categories, but check its grouping against the original posts.
2. **Learning log repo (ongoing).** Create a public GitHub repo called `learning-log`. Each week, write a few lines on what you learned, what confused you and what you built. After a year, this becomes evidence of steady growth, which is exactly what employers want to see.
3. **Job market scraper (stretch).** Write a small script that collects entry-level job titles from one public job board, where its terms allow it, and charts which skills come up most often each month. It gives you practice with HTTP, parsing, data storage and a chart, and it produces a real insight.

## Sri Lanka notes

- Degree programmes differ a lot. Some include a compulsory industrial placement, often in Year 3, and some don't. Find out how *your* programme handles internships early, because it shapes your timeline. The Internships guide covers this (coming soon).
- Don't rule yourself out because you are not in Colombo. Many teams work in a hybrid way, and remote-first employers care about your work, not where you live.
- English communication is a real differentiator in client-facing export companies. If this is a weak area, start working on it now.
